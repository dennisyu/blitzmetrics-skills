# Business Brand Strategist

*Part of the Local Service Spotlight Agent Skill Pack — Company Edition.*

**Use this when** you're starting (or resetting) your company's market position and need to name it before you build anything. Step 1 of the Local Service Spotlight method — the brief every other skill aims at.

## Inputs
- What AI already knows about the company. Paste the output of asking ChatGPT/Claude: *"What do you know about [company] in [your city]? Write a candid profile — what it does, who it serves, how it differs. Be honest, not flattering."*
- The company's website, Google Business Profile, and 5–10 recent posts or ads.
- The job record: biggest jobs, the work your crews would do all day, the customers you want more of.

## Steps
1. **Separate fact from aspiration.** Two columns — provable today vs. what you wish were true. Only fact earns authority; aspiration becomes the plan.
2. **Name your ideal customers** — the work you want more of: service lines, job size, customer type (homeowner, commercial, property manager), and the coverage area you actually serve. A vague target makes a vague brand.
3. **Name the service category** you intend to own — "[category] in [your city]" — narrow enough to win, big enough to matter.
4. **Write the differentiation in ONE sentence** — what your company does or guarantees that direct competitors don't and can't easily copy. If it could sit on a competitor's site, it isn't differentiation yet.
5. **Lock the 5 proof points** from real jobs that back you to someone who Googles the company before calling — results, named customers or partners, review counts, credentials, lighthouse ties.
6. **Pressure-test the brief.** Read it as a customer comparing three quotes. If it doesn't say why this company, specifically, in 30 seconds, tighten it until it does.

## Output
- Ideal customers: 3–5 bullets, concrete enough to route leads from — the work you want more of.
- Service category + coverage area: one line.
- Differentiation: 1 sentence only this company can sign.
- Top-5 proof points from real jobs, strongest first.
- Authority scorecard baseline: today's score per claim — proof in hand vs. none — the number the other nine skills exist to move.
- "What's still missing" gap list — the assignment for downstream skills.

## Definition of done
- The ideal-customer definition names service lines, job size, customer type, and coverage area; a stranger could route the right lead from it.
- The differentiation fails the swap test: put a competitor's name on the sentence and it reads false.
- Every proof point is checkable — a link, a name, a number.
- The gap list is honest. A gap isn't embarrassing; an unnamed gap is.

## Worked examples
- **HVAC Quote (Marko Sipila):** ideal customers = HVAC owners who hate quoting; differentiation = the AI quoting company whose founder shoots phone interviews at industry conferences. One brief drove the content that drove 300+ customers.
- **Superior Fence & Rail:** the largest US fence franchise (110+ locations, $310M+) still runs on a sharp brief — it's why customers and franchisees seek the company out by name, not just by category.
- **NaiL AI (George Paladichuk):** picked one claimable category — AI for roofing — before anyone else named it, and became the reference point.

## For marketing managers & owners
**If you're the marketing manager:** this brief is the strategy deliverable an agency would charge a retainer for — positioning, proof, and a scorecard baseline you produce in an afternoon and every later skill executes against.
**If you own the business:** a company known for specific work wins bigger jobs, better hires, and premium pricing; a company known vaguely competes on price. This is where you stop being "a [category] company" and become THE one.
**Your edge:** write the one sentence only your company can sign — the guarantee, track record, specialty, or coverage nobody else in your market could put on their own site.

## Run on a persistent agent (Fable 5)
- **Loop to done:** run the brief against its own Definition of done — swap test, checkable proof points, honest gap list — and rewrite until every line passes, not until it reads well.
- **Self-verify:** pressure-test as the customer comparing three quotes (step 6) on every loop, not just the last one.
- **Compound with memory:** this is step 1 — write the finished brief to memory so the other nine skills aim at it without re-asking; on a rerun, read the prior brief and update it instead of starting blank.
- **Log the run:** save each brief version as a meta-article example — what changed between versions and why is positioning data.

See `boil-the-ocean.md` for the full operating principles.

## Notes — Dennis's method
- The ideal-customer definition matters most: it's literally the work you want more of, so make it concrete enough to act on.
- This brief becomes the aim (the "G/Goals" in GCT) for every downstream skill: proof harvest → site → Knowledge Panel → Dollar a Day.
- Don't flatter the company; the market rewards a true, narrow claim over a broad, hopeful one.
- Boil the ocean on inputs, not adjectives — ten real data points from the job record beat one clever tagline.
